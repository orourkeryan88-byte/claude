The rule that saves you

Never launch traffic into a downstream you haven't checked. If the offer is weak, the margin is thin, or nobody answers the phone when a lead comes in, more traffic makes the client poorer and the failure gets attributed to you. Say it before launch, in writing.

02 Intake questionnaire

Send to client

Build it as a form — Tally, Typeform, Google Forms. Target 15–20 minutes; longer and it doesn't come back. The starred questions are the ones that actually change what you build.

Business basics

  • Legal and trading name · website URLs
  • What do you sell, in one sentence, as a customer would say it?
  • Years trading, team size, revenue band
  • Who has final sign-off? (name, email, phone) · who else must be copied?

The offer

  • Products/services and price points · your most profitable offer
  • Average order value, gross margin, and lifetime value — and how you calculated LTV
  • Guarantee, trial, or risk reversal
  • The #1 objection people raise before buying

The customer

  • Describe your best customer and the trigger that makes them start looking
  • What are they using instead of you right now — including "nothing"?
  • Paste 3–5 real reviews, quotes or support tickets, verbatim. Don't paraphrase. This is where the ad copy comes from.

Numbers and targets

  • Current spend by channel · current cost per lead and per acquisition · monthly volume
  • What number defines success in 90 days? "35 qualified calls a month under $180 each" beats "more leads."
  • The most you can pay to acquire a customer and still be happy

What's been tried

  • Channels run before and what happened · previous agencies and what went wrong
  • What's working right now that we should not touch?
  • Off-limits: claims, competitors, channels, creative styles

Sales and fulfilment

  • Walk through what happens after a lead comes in, step by step
  • Who follows up, how fast, on what channel · lead-to-sale close rate
  • Capacity ceiling — how many new customers a month before quality drops?

Brand and practicalities

  • Brand guidelines, photo/video assets, testimonials — links
  • Tone of voice in three words · compliance constraints
  • Who approves creative and how fast · preferred meeting slot and timezone
  • Anything we should know that we didn't ask

Score it the moment it lands

Rate 1–5: offer strength · margin headroom · data quality · sales follow-up speed · creative asset supply. Any score of 1 or 2 becomes a Week 1 fix in the roadmap. These five scores predict the account's outcome better than anything you'll do in the ad manager.

Six questions to always ask out loud

  1. If we could only move one number in 90 days, which one and why?
  2. What would make you say in six months that this was the best money you spent?
  3. What would make you fire us?
  4. Who inside your business is sceptical about hiring an agency?
  5. How fast can you really turn around creative approval — honestly?
  6. If we brought you 3× the leads next month, what breaks?

Send a written recap within 24 hours: agreed KPI and target, confirmed budget, decisions, open questions, actions with owners and dates. State in the email that silence counts as agreement.

05 Welcome packet

Send to client

The document that prevents 80% of future friction. Most client conflict is an expectations gap, and this is where you close it before it opens.

What we need from you

  1. Approvals within 2 business days. Creative sitting in a queue is the single biggest cause of slow results.
  2. Five-minute response to inbound leads in business hours. This moves conversion more than any targeting change available to us.
  3. Honest numbers. Tell us what actually closed. Platform data flatters; your bank account doesn't.
  4. One decision-maker. Committee approval kills momentum.
  5. Raw material. Photos, video, testimonials. The account is only as good as the creative we get to feed it.

What you get from us

  • A written weekly update whether the news is good or bad.
  • Reports in plain English — spend, leads, cost per lead, sales, return.
  • We tell you something isn't working before you have to ask.
  • We recommend against spending when spending isn't the answer.

Timeline reality check — put this in writing early

Weeks 1–2: setup, tracking, creative, launch. No meaningful performance data yet.
Weeks 3–6: learning phase. Costs are highest and noisiest here. This is normal.
Weeks 7–12: optimisation. This is where the numbers should start looking like the plan.

Judging paid media on week-two data is the most expensive mistake a client can make. Agreeing this in week one is how you survive week three.

Escalation

Unhappy? Tell the account lead first. Not resolved in three business days? Escalate to the founder, with a phone number. Say plainly: we would much rather hear it early and awkwardly than late and politely.

07 The funnel blueprint

The framework

Five stages, one job each. Most agencies obsess over stage two and lose the client's money at stages four and five.

Stage 1 · Attention

Cold: paid social, paid search, YouTube, organic. Warm: retargeting visitors, video viewers, engagers. Hot: brand search, list, referrals. Start at 70 / 20 / 10 and shift toward warm as the pixel matures.

Diagnostic: low CTR is a creative or audience problem, not a bidding problem.

Stage 2 · Interest — pick one entry point, never two on a page

Entry pointBest forTypical conversion
Lead magnetHigh-consideration B2B and services20–40%
Quiz / assessmentProducts needing diagnosis or variety30–50%
Free trial / sampleSaaS, subscription, ecom5–15%
Book a callHigh ticket, $3k+2–8%
Direct to productLow ticket, impulse1–4%

Landing page anatomy, in order

  1. Headline — the outcome, not the product
  2. Subhead — who it's for and the mechanism
  3. Proof above the fold — a number, a logo row, or a face
  4. Three benefit blocks in customer language
  5. Testimonials with names and specifics
  6. Offer stack, itemised
  7. Risk reversal
  8. FAQ that kills the top five objections
  9. One call to action, repeated three or four times, never competing with a second

Diagnostic: cheap clicks but expensive leads means the ad promised something the page doesn't deliver.

Stage 3 · Conversion

The offer equation

Value = (Dream outcome × Perceived likelihood) ÷ (Time to result × Effort required)

Four levers. Most businesses only ever pull price — the one lever that damages the business.

Assets: sales page or call script · booking page with SMS and email reminders (no-shows destroy high-ticket funnels) · checkout with guest option, wallets and three fields maximum · order bump (+10–30% AOV) and one-click upsell (+15–40% AOV) · abandonment recovery, three emails and one SMS over 72 hours.

Diagnostic: cheap leads that don't become sales is an offer or sales-process problem. Do not try to fix it with more traffic.

Stage 4 · Follow-up — where the money actually is

Contact inbound leads within five minutes. Conversion falls off a cliff after that, and this one change routinely beats any targeting work you could do instead.

  • New lead, 7 emails over 14 days: deliver → story → objection one → proof → objection two → offer → deadline
  • No-show recovery: instant rebook link, then day 1 and day 3
  • Post-purchase, 5 emails: confirm → set expectations → onboard → ask for review → cross-sell
  • Long nurture: weekly value, monthly offer

Stage 5 · Expansion

Referral ask at peak satisfaction · reactivation of dormant buyers · subscription or retainer conversion · cross-sell on purchase history · win-back at 60, 90 and 180 days.

Target LTV:CAC of 3:1 or better. Below 2:1 the business cannot scale on paid media and no amount of media-buying skill fixes that.

Funnel diagnostic

SymptomLikely causeFix
High CPM, low CTRWeak creative or wrong audienceNew hooks, new angles
High CTR, low page conversionAd-to-page mismatchMatch the page headline to the ad promise
Good leads, no salesOffer or follow-upFix speed-to-lead and the offer, not the ads
Sales but no profitBroken economicsRaise price, raise AOV, or cut CAC
Works, then dies at week 3Creative fatigueRefresh on a schedule, not in a panic

09 Offer & messaging

The framework

Mine the language before writing a word

Sources: their reviews and their competitors', support tickets, sales call recordings, forums, and the verbatim quotes from the intake. Tag every line into Pain · Desire · Objection · Vocabulary. Ad copy gets assembled from these, not invented.

Angles, not adjectives

An angle is a reason to care, not a description. Write at least five before you write a single ad:

  1. Problem/agitate — name the pain sharply
  2. Mechanism — why this works when the other things didn't
  3. Identity — "for people who…"
  4. Comparison — versus the obvious alternative
  5. Proof-led — a specific result for a specific customer
  6. Contrarian — challenge a belief the market holds

Test angles before you test colours, thumbnails or button copy. Angle is worth roughly ten times the delta of any design tweak.

The one-page message brief

Fill one per client, before any creative: Who (in their words) · Problem (as they'd describe it to a friend) · Promise (specific, time-bound) · Mechanism · Proof (three strongest pieces) · Objections (top five, each with a counter) · Call to action (one, unambiguous).

Headline formulas that survive contact with reality

  • Get [outcome] in [timeframe] without [the thing they dread]
  • The [category] for [specific person] who [specific situation]
  • [Number] [customers] used this to [result]
  • Stop [painful activity]. Start [desired state].

10 Creative testing

The framework

Creative is the highest-leverage variable in paid media. Targeting is largely automated now; the message is not.

The 3 × 3 × 3 cadence

Every round ships 3 angles × 3 hooks × 3 formats. Never test one ad against one ad — you'll wait weeks for significance that never arrives.

  • Angles — the reason to care
  • Hooks — the first three seconds or first line
  • Formats — UGC talking head, static with text, demo, before/after, founder-to-camera, testimonial

Rules

  1. One variable per comparison. Change the hook and the offer together and you've learned nothing.
  2. Fund it properly. Roughly 50 conversions per variant before calling a winner, or 3–5× target CPA in spend before killing one.
  3. Kill on a rule, not a feeling — e.g. cut at 3× target CPA with zero conversions.
  4. Winners get scaled, then iterated — same angle, new hook. Don't retire what works.
  5. Refresh on a schedule. Frequency past ~2.5–3.0 in a 7-day window, or CTR down 30% from peak, means fatigue has arrived. Plan for it instead of reacting.

The creative brief — one per concept

Angle and the belief it targets · the exact hook · three body points in order · proof element · call to action · format, ratio, duration · and what you expect to learn if it wins and if it loses. That last line is what separates testing from guessing.

The asset that compounds

Keep every ad you've ever run with its spend, CPA, CTR, hook rate and a one-line note on why you think it did what it did. This library is the real agency asset — it's what makes month six cheaper than month one, for every client you take on afterwards.

Attribution honesty

  • Platforms over-report, GA4 under-reports, the client's CRM and bank are the truth.
  • Show platform numbers and client-confirmed sales side by side. Explain the gap once, in plain English, then keep the same method every month.
  • Lock a UTM convention on day one and never change it mid-quarter.
  • Add "how did you hear about us?" to forms. Imperfect, but it catches what pixels miss.

The monthly report

Page one must be readable by someone who knows nothing about advertising: spend, results, cost per result, revenue, return — plus one paragraph on what happened, why, and what you're doing about it. Then: what we did (concrete actions, not activity theatre) · what we learned (winning and losing creative, and what each ruled out) · what's blocked (including anything waiting on the client, by name and date) · next month's three priorities, each with an owner, a date, and the number it should move.

The "what's blocked" section is the one that earns trust. Write it even when it's uncomfortable — especially then.

14 Retention & saves

Check weekly

Clients rarely leave over a bad month. They leave over a bad month plus silence.

SignalSeverity
Approvals slipping past five daysMedium
Client stops replying in the channelHigh
A new stakeholder appears asking "what are we paying for?"High
Client asks for a breakdown of hoursHigh
Two monthly calls missed in a rowHigh
Results flat for six weeks with no test velocityCritical
"Reviewing options" or "internal restructure"Critical

The save play — within 48 hours of a critical signal

  1. Call, don't email. No agenda deck.
  2. Ask, then shut up. "Be straight with me — where is this falling short?" Take notes. Do not defend.
  3. Reflect it back in writing the same day, unedited.
  4. Bring a plan within three business days: three specific changes, dates, and the number each should move. Include something at no charge if the fault is yours.
  5. Set a 30-day checkpoint with an explicit success definition — and honour it, including walking away if it isn't met.

When to fire a client

They ask you to make claims that aren't true · they're abusive toward your team (no revenue justifies it) · chronic non-payment · their offer or fulfilment is broken and they refuse to fix it, because no amount of traffic saves that and the failure will be attributed to you.

Exit cleanly: written notice, full handover, access removed, final invoice, no bad-mouthing. Ex-clients refer.

Renewal and expansion

  • Day 60 — ask for the testimonial while the win is fresh.
  • Day 75 — present the next quarter's roadmap before renewal comes up, so renewal becomes a conversation about the plan rather than a referendum on the past.
  • Expansion only when the primary KPI is being hit consistently, capacity headroom exists, and the second channel has a clear thesis. Never upsell into an underperforming account.
